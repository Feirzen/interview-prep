DATE: 2026-10-13
TYPE: Listening

COACH: Day two. This one's a listening day. No pressure, just background you'll want in your pocket when an interviewer asks what you know about the profession. There's one quick practice question at the very end.
COACH: Let's start with where this whole thing came from.
COACH: In the mid nineteen sixties, the United States had a real shortage of anesthesia providers. Three anesthesiologists, all academic department chairs, looked at that problem and did something pretty clever. Their names were Joachim Gravenstein, John Steinhaus, and Perry Volpitto. Instead of just saying "we need more anesthesiologists," they broke anesthesia care down into its pieces. What tasks does this job involve, what education does each one need, and what level of responsibility does each one carry. From that analysis came the idea of a highly trained provider who would come from a pre-medical background and work under the direction of an anesthesiologist, as part of a team.
COACH: The first two programs opened in nineteen sixty-nine. One at Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, and one at Emory, in Atlanta. Steinhaus was at Emory. So when you walk into Emory's building, you're walking into one of the two places where this profession started. Their website says "leading the way for fifty years," and that's not marketing fluff. It's literally true.
COACH: That's worth remembering, and it's worth mentioning if it fits naturally. Something like, "It means something to me to train at one of the programs that started the profession." Don't overdo it. One sentence.
COACH: Fast forward. In nineteen eighty-nine the National Commission for Certification of Anesthesiologist Assistants was formed. That's the N C C A A. It runs the certifying exam, which is administered through the National Board of Medical Examiners, the same organization behind the medical boards. Today every program is a master's program, affiliated with a medical school and accredited through CAAHEP. Students finish with at least two thousand clinical hours and around six hundred cases before graduation.
COACH: After you're certified, you maintain it with forty hours of continuing medical education every two years, and a recertification exam at regular intervals. In other words, the learning doesn't stop at graduation, and you should say you're glad about that.
COACH: Now, the heart of it. The anesthesia care team model. The A C T model. You need to be able to explain this simply, because there's a decent chance someone asks you to.
COACH: In the care team model, a physician anesthesiologist leads. Under that physician, you have non-physician anesthetists. That's C A As and C R N As, nurse anesthetists, plus residents at teaching hospitals. The anesthesiologist medically directs several rooms at once. Historically, the model was built around one anesthesiologist directing up to four rooms. The actual ratio depends on the state and on billing rules. Medicare, for example, requires the anesthesiologist to be immediately available.
COACH: In practice, it looks like what you saw. The anesthesiologist and the C A A meet the patient and agree on a plan. The anesthesiologist is present for the critical moments, like induction. Then the C A A stays in the room for the whole case, managing the anesthetic minute to minute, while the anesthesiologist moves between rooms, checks in, covers breaks, and comes back for anything that needs them. You saw an attending covering two rooms in July, and three rooms on August thirty-first. You saw Karissa handle emergence on her own while her attending was in another room. That's the model.
COACH: What does a C A A actually do? Pre-anesthetic evaluation, taking a history and doing an exam. Inducing and maintaining anesthesia. Airway management, including intubation. Placing lines and setting up advanced monitoring. Regional anesthesia under the anesthesiologist's direction. Emergence, handoff to recovery, and post-op checks. Basically the full arc of a case, every day.
COACH: Now, the comparison you'll definitely think about, and might get asked about. C A As versus C R N As.
COACH: Here's the clean version. Within the care team, the American Society of Anesthesiologists says C A As and C R N As have the same scope. They do the same work, they cover the same cases, and they relieve each other for breaks. There's a well-known study from twenty eighteen, published in the journal Anesthesiology, by Sun and colleagues. It compared care teams that used C A As with care teams that used C R N As. They found no significant differences in mortality, length of stay, or spending. Same outcomes.
COACH: The difference is the path, and the practice setting. C R N As come up through nursing. They work as I C U nurses, then do a doctoral nurse anesthesia program. C A As come through a pre-medical undergrad, then a master's program, and their training is designed by anesthesiologists, for the physician-led team. And C A As always practice within that team, under an anesthesiologist. In some states, C R N As can also practice on their own.
COACH: There's real politics here. The nurse anesthetist professional organization has historically opposed C A A licensure when it comes up in new states. You might get asked about it. The right tone is respectful and boring. Never trash C R N As. You'll work next to them for your whole career. Something like: "From what I've seen, the best rooms are the ones where everyone's excellent at their job and knows their role. I want to be part of a team, and that's what the C A A role is built for."
COACH: Where's the profession now? According to the American Academy of Anesthesiologist Assistants, as of this fall, C A As can practice in twenty-two states, plus Washington D C and Guam. It keeps growing. Nevada authorized C A As in twenty twenty-three. Virginia and Tennessee both came in twenty twenty-five. Washington State adopted practice rules that same year. New programs keep opening too, like Lipscomb in Nashville and Kansas City University in Joplin.
COACH: And Georgia is one of the profession's strongholds. It's a licensure state, it has two programs, Emory and South University in Savannah, and Atlanta has a big C A A workforce. That's not true where you're from. In Utah there are three practicing C A As, and you shadowed two of them. That contrast is a nice detail to keep in your back pocket.
COACH: A couple of career realities, so you sound informed. The pay is strong. The C A A you shadowed in July said the University of Utah pays on the lower end, in the two thirty to two fifty range, and other markets pay more. You don't need to bring up money in an interview, and you shouldn't lead with it. But it's good to know. Lifestyle varies. Some people work regular hospital schedules with call. Some, like Karissa, do travel or locums work, moving around the country eight to ten times a year. The profession is flexible.
COACH: One more thing. The weird part of the job. You saw it. Hours of steady vigilance, then a few minutes where everything matters. Interviewers love it when you show you understand that rhythm. Your cautery story is perfect for this. The monitor showed artifact, and Karissa wasn't treating the monitor, she was interpreting it.
COACH: Okay, let's lock it in. Three things you should be able to say in one breath. One: the profession started in nineteen sixty-nine at Emory and Case Western, to solve a provider shortage with a team. Two: the care team is physician-led, and C A As and C R N As do the same work inside it, with the same outcomes. Three: the profession is growing state by state, and Georgia is one of its homes.
COACH: Now, one practice question. This is one of the best ones you could get, because you've already watched someone answer it for real. Picture the husband from August thirty-first. Your proctor is going to play a family member.
PROCTOR: I'm sorry, I'm a little confused. You're not the doctor? So who's actually doing my wife's anesthesia today?
PAUSE 60
COACH: Here's what Karissa did, and it's what you want to do. Answer directly, no defensiveness, in plain words. "I'm a certified anesthesiologist assistant. I'll be with your wife the whole time she's asleep. Dr. so-and-so is the anesthesiologist leading her care. He's overseeing a few rooms today, he'll be here for the important parts, and one of us is with her at every moment." Then check that they understood. If an interviewer asks you to explain the care team model, this exact answer works. Just say it to them instead.
