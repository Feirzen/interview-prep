COACH: Day seven. Today and tomorrow are about owning your application. Interviewers will have read it, and some will pick a line and ask you about it. Today is your shadowing, which is the most likely thing they'll dig into, because it's the best evidence you understand the job.
COACH: Let's walk through what you've seen, so it's fresh. You have three experiences. The heart transplant, years ago. About twenty hours with C A As at the University of Utah in July. And about ten hours on August thirty-first with Karissa. Know those numbers roughly, in case someone asks how much you've shadowed. Around thirty hours with C A As, plus the transplant.
COACH: July, at the University of Utah. A dental O R. Two cases worth remembering.
COACH: The first patient was a forty-year-old man with cerebral palsy, who couldn't cooperate with a normal induction. They started with an I M ketamine injection to calm him, and the first dose barely touched him. The team split up. The C A A got consent from his parents while the anesthesiologist talked the patient through the shot. Because it was dental surgery, they intubated through the nose, to keep the tube out of the surgical field, using a video laryngoscope and Magill forceps. His large tongue made it harder. Then, near the end, the anesthesiologist couldn't be there for wake-up. So about fifteen minutes before emergence, they briefed the surgeon, and the C A A and the circulating nurse handled the whole wake-up and handoff themselves.
COACH: The second patient was a twenty-eight-year-old man, autistic and needle-phobic. His case had been cancelled once before because he became combative. This time he asked for a blood draw while he was asleep, so he wouldn't have to face a needle awake. That's a great small detail. Anesthesia isn't just for surgery. It's how you make care possible for someone who couldn't otherwise get it. That fits your thread perfectly. People who can't fully care for themselves.
COACH: That intubation was hard too. It took three people. The anesthesiologist held the scope, the C A A guided the tube with forceps, and a third provider pushed rocuronium to relax the cords. When the blood pressure dropped, she gave phenylephrine without missing a beat. And she gave antibiotics before the surgeon asked, because she saw that the extractions were coming next.
COACH: And the ephedrine case. From your personal statement. The colostomy repair, pressure in the seventies, the alarm going off, and the C A A holding the ephedrine because she knew the incision would bring the pressure up. That's your signature moment. You've practiced it already.
COACH: August thirty-first, with Karissa. Breast surgical oncology. Three cases under general anesthesia. Her attending was covering three rooms.
COACH: The moments to remember. The patient's husband asking, "so who's the anesthesiologist," and Karissa explaining the care team at the bedside. Karissa planning a supraglottic airway, her attending suggesting video laryngoscopy, and Karissa switching without argument. The electrocautery putting artifact on the E E G and heart rate monitors, and Karissa interpreting the numbers instead of treating them. And at emergence, the surgeon calling out that the patient was coughing, and Karissa already pushing propofol.
COACH: Now, three cautions. These matter, because a knowledgeable interviewer will catch them.
COACH: Caution one. In your notes from August, the first patient's propranolol was held for five days before surgery. Don't repeat that detail. Current guidance is to continue beta blockers through surgery, because stopping them suddenly can cause rebound fast heart rate and high blood pressure. Something got lost in translation that day. If you mention her history at all, keep it general. Hypertension and diabetes.
COACH: Caution two. Those cases were listed as possible axillary node dissections. From what you saw, they stopped at sentinel node biopsy. Describe them that way. Lumpectomies with sentinel node biopsy.
COACH: Caution three. This one is actually an opportunity. Your personal statement ends a paragraph with, "It may be the surgeon's operation, but it is the anesthesia team's patient." Someone may ask about that line. And here's the thing. On August thirty-first, Karissa's attending pushed back on exactly that idea. He called the relationship symbiotic. The surgeons bring the patients, and they know them long before anesthesia does. In the moment of the case, though, the framing is partly right.
COACH: So if they ask about that line, you get to show maturity. Something like: "I wrote that after my first shadowing. Then an attending pushed back on it, and I think he's right that it's more of a partnership. The surgeon has known that patient for weeks. But during the case, the anesthesia team is responsible for keeping them alive, so I still think the line holds in that moment." That answer shows you take feedback and think in nuance. Interviewers love that.
COACH: Let's practice.
PROCTOR: Tell me about your shadowing experience.
PAUSE 90
COACH: Check. Did you give a rough sense of hours and settings? Did you tell one specific moment, rather than listing everything? And did you say what you learned about the role, not just what you watched? The best answers here end on an insight. For example: explaining the care team model is part of practicing in it.
PROCTOR: What surprised you most when you shadowed?
PAUSE 75
COACH: Good honest options. How quiet and steady most of the case is, and then how fast it moves at induction and emergence. Or that the family didn't know what a C A A was, so patient education is part of the job. Or how much the C A A ran the room independently, with the anesthesiologist checking in. Pick one. Make it something real.
PROCTOR: In your personal statement, you wrote that it may be the surgeon's operation, but it's the anesthesia team's patient. Can you tell me more about what you meant?
PAUSE 90
COACH: Did you use the attending's pushback? That's the move. You stand by the line in the moment of the case, and you add the nuance that it's really a partnership. That's a much better answer than just defending the line.
PROCTOR: Walk me through what the C A A did during one case you observed, from start to finish.
PAUSE 90
COACH: This tests whether you were paying attention. A good walk-through: the pre-op conversation and consent, the plan agreed with the attending, induction and the airway, maintenance, which was propofol and remifentanil in many of your cases, watching the field and anticipating things like antibiotics, the attending checking in, then emergence and handoff. You don't need drug doses. You need the flow. If you got the order right, you understand the job.
