COACH: Story four. The avoided patient. This is your integrity and escalation story.
COACH: Recap. A provider was avoiding a frequent-complaint patient by sending everything through the nurses. It wasn't your place to correct a doctor. So instead of staying quiet or gossiping, you raised your concern through the proper channel, your supervisor.
COACH: Honest assessment. This is your weakest story, and that's fine. Every bank has one. Here's how to handle it. Use it only when they ask specifically about integrity, ethics, or speaking up. Keep it short, about a minute. And frame it entirely around your judgment, not around the doctor's failure. The story is about how you decided what to do, not about a bad doctor.
COACH: The framing sounds like this. "I noticed something that made me uncomfortable about how a patient was getting care. I had three options. Say nothing, talk about it with coworkers, or bring it to the right person. It wasn't my role to correct a physician, so I brought it to my supervisor, and let them handle it from there."
COACH: Don't describe the doctor's personality. Don't say the patient was annoying. And if you know what happened afterward, say it briefly. If you don't, that's okay. What you learned is that escalating through the right channel protects the patient and keeps the team intact.
COACH: The bridge is strong, actually. In the care team, escalation has a clear path. Concerns go to the anesthesiologist. You already respect that structure.
COACH: Story five. The five-day housing scramble. This is your teamwork-under-pressure story, and it's an A.
COACH: Recap. As mission housing coordinator, you'd get five days' notice to house four missionaries in an area with no home, often without knowing exactly how many were coming or when. You got the fixed requirements from the mission president. You mobilized nearby missionaries and local leaders to search. You coordinated furniture and transport. You kept the president updated at every step. And when the timing slipped, you built backup housing using travel funds.
COACH: The key line, and you already have it, is this: you acted on what was certain instead of freezing over what wasn't. Say that sentence, almost word for word. It's excellent.
COACH: What to tighten. Pick one specific time. Even if you did this many times, telling one specific scramble makes it real. And it was in Ecuador, in Spanish, with local leaders. Mention that, because it shows you can lead across language and culture.
COACH: The bridge. In anesthesia you're constantly deciding with incomplete information. The surgeon might take an hour or three. Blood loss might be fifty milliliters or a liter. You plan for what you know, prepare for what you don't, and keep the team informed. That's this story.
COACH: Story six. The two hundred dollar deficit. This is your failure story. Every interview has a failure question, and this is a good answer, because it's a real mistake with a real fix.
COACH: Recap. While running mission finances in a chaotic period, you covered some expenses with your own money, planning to pay yourself back. You lost track on paper and ended up two hundred dollars short, no matter how you ran the numbers. You told the mission president the moment he was free. Together you figured out what caused it. And you set two rules. A system rule: never use personal funds again. And a personal rule: delegate before you hit overload.
COACH: Why it works. You own it right away. You report it fast. You fix the system, not just the moment. And you learned something about yourself. That's exactly what interviewers want in a failure story.
COACH: Two cautions. One, keep the money details simple and leave the tithing piece out, like you planned. Two, don't make it sound too small. The point isn't the two hundred dollars. The point is that something you were responsible for didn't add up, and you reported it immediately instead of hiding it.
COACH: The bridge is powerful for anesthesia, so use it. "In anesthesia, that same rule applies to medication errors and controlled substance counts. If something doesn't add up, you report it the minute you know. I learned that with two hundred dollars, which is a cheap way to learn it."
COACH: And bonus. This story also answers the weakness question. Your weakness is taking on too much before you delegate. Story six is the proof that you know it and you've built a fix.
