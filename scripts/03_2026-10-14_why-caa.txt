COACH: Day three. Today's question is going to come up at Emory. I'd bet on it. Why anesthesia, and why a C A A specifically?
COACH: Here's what interviewers are actually listening for. First, that the reason is specific, and comes from something you saw or did, not a general idea about helping people. Second, that you understand the role, including the care team. Third, that this is a choice and not a fallback. Fourth, that you know what the hard parts are and want the job anyway.
COACH: Your answer has three layers. Let's build them one at a time.
COACH: Layer one. Why anesthesia. Your strongest material is the ephedrine moment from your shadowing. Quick refresher. A patient's blood pressure dropped at the start of a colostomy repair. Systolic in the seventies. The alarm went off. The C A A had ephedrine drawn up, and she didn't give it. She leaned over and told you to watch what happens when the surgeon makes the first cut. The surgeon called the incision, and the pressure came right back up to one ten over seventy. She'd expected the surgical stimulus to trigger a sympathetic response.
COACH: Why that moment matters is the real answer. It's physiology happening live, and someone who understood it well enough to trust it over an alarm. You studied that physiology in exercise science. You saw the same kind of judgment in detox, where a rising COWS score didn't automatically mean another Subutex dose, because what mattered was where the patient was heading. That's what draws you to anesthesia. Real-time physiology, and the judgment to act on where the patient is going.
COACH: Layer two. Why the C A A role. Two pieces. One, the responsibility thread. Under anesthesia, a patient can't do anything for themselves. Every part of their safety is on the team. That's the most complete version of what you've been doing as a caregiver and an interpreter. Two, the care team itself. You like the idea of giving one patient your full attention, with a physician backing you up who's a second set of eyes whenever you need it. Use your words from the personal statement. Continuous, singular attention, backed by a second set of eyes.
COACH: Layer three. Why not something else. This is where people get nervous, but you're in a great spot here, because you can be honest. You were on a P A track for years, and they'll see that on your resume. Own it. Don't hide it.
COACH: Here's the shape. "I spent years building toward P A, and I'm glad I did, because that's how I got all my patient care experience. But P A is broad, and when I started shadowing in anesthesia, I realized the part of medicine I love most is the physiology and pharmacology happening in the moment. P A would have kept me near that. C A A puts me right in it."
COACH: For C R N A, the honest answer is about the path. That route goes through a nursing degree and years of I C U work. You came up the pre-med path, and C A A is the role designed for exactly that background, built by anesthesiologists for the physician-led team. That team is the model you actually want to work in.
COACH: And for medicine, if they ask why not be the anesthesiologist. Keep it simple and positive. You want to be the one in the room, managing the anesthetic minute to minute, and you want to get there in two years, not twelve. And you genuinely like being on a team where someone with more training has your back. Never make it sound like medical school was too hard. Make it sound like this role fits you better.
COACH: Alright. Proctor, first question.
PROCTOR: Let's start simple. Why anesthesia?
PAUSE 75
COACH: Check yourself. Did you tell the ephedrine story, or at least a piece of it? Did you name the physiology, the sympathetic response to incision? And did you connect it to yourself? The story is only half the answer. The other half is why it grabbed you specifically.
PROCTOR: Okay. But why an anesthesiologist assistant, specifically?
PAUSE 75
COACH: The two pieces were the responsibility thread and the care team. If you only talked about anesthesia, you answered the last question twice. The C A A part is the team. Full attention on one patient, with a physician backing you up. Bonus points if you used a real moment, like the attending stepping into the room mid-case to ask whether the C A A needed anything.
PROCTOR: I noticed a lot of your experience is aimed at P A school. Is this your backup plan?
PAUSE 75
COACH: This is the one to nail, because it's a little bit of a challenge. The tone you want is relaxed and honest, not defensive. Something like: "No. Honestly, it's the other way around. P A was the plan before I knew this job existed. Once I shadowed, it wasn't close." Then give one specific reason. The physiology in real time, or the team. And here's a real point in your favor. You're applying only to C A A programs now. You can say so plainly. "This is the only thing I'm applying to." That's a strong signal.
PROCTOR: What do you think will be the hardest part of this job for you?
PAUSE 75
COACH: Good answers pick something real and show you've thought about it. A few options for you. The rhythm: long stretches of vigilance, and then moments where everything happens at once. Staying sharp through the quiet part is a skill. Or being responsible for a patient who can't tell you anything is wrong, so all your information comes from monitors and physiology, and the monitors can lie, like the cautery artifact you saw. Or the pace of the program itself. Pick one, say why, and say what you'd do about it. Don't say you can't think of anything hard.
PROCTOR: Last one. As a C A A, you'll always practice under an anesthesiologist. How do you feel about never working independently?
PAUSE 75
COACH: This is a gift of a question, because you've seen the answer. On August thirty-first, Karissa planned a supraglottic airway. Her attending recommended video laryngoscopy instead. She disagreed a little on the merits, and she switched anyway, without arguing, because extra caution cost nothing. And later, at emergence, she was already treating the patient before the surgeon even said anything, with her attending in another room. So the autonomy is real, moment to moment. And the backup is real too. You can say: "That's the version of autonomy I actually want." That's a great line. Use it.
COACH: Day three, done. If you only remember one thing: specific story, the care team, and a choice, not a fallback. Tomorrow is all about Emory itself, so you'll know exactly where you're walking into. See you then.
