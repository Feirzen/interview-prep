COACH: Day four. Today is all Emory. What the program is, how it's built, what the numbers say, what your interview day will look like, and what you should ask them. Two practice questions at the end.
COACH: Let's start with the basics. The degree is a Master of Medical Science in Anesthesiology. Emory calls it a concentrated graduate medical education program, run inside the Emory University School of Medicine, in the Department of Anesthesiology. You'd start in August twenty twenty-seven and finish in December twenty twenty-nine. That's seven semesters, twenty-seven continuous months. Continuous is the key word. There's no summer off.
COACH: The program isn't on Emory's main campus. It's at fifty-seven Executive Park South, Suite three hundred, off Interstate eighty-five, a few miles from main campus. The invitation even says, in bold, don't go to the main campus on Clifton Road. Remember that the morning of.
COACH: Now, how the twenty-seven months are built. The first four semesters mix coursework, hands-on skills labs, and human patient simulation, with semester-long clinical rotations in metro Atlanta running alongside. By the end of semester four, students have at least five hundred clinical hours. So you're in the O R early, while you're still learning the science.
COACH: That's worth noticing, because it's different from some programs. From your shadowing in July, you learned that N S U holds off on pairing classroom work with O R time until the second year. Emory puts you in the O R while you're in class. If that appeals to you, and it should, because you learn by applying things, that's a real reason to want Emory. Just don't knock another program to say it. Say what you like about Emory's approach, not what you dislike about anyone else's.
COACH: Semesters five through seven, the final twelve months, are full-time clinical rotations, with monthly peer-led seminars. Those rotations happen in metro Atlanta and at out-of-town sites. There are required rotations in cardiac, obstetric, and pediatric anesthesia, along with general surgery and a variety of other options. The clinical sites include Emory-owned hospitals, major affiliates, academic medical centers, and private practice settings.
COACH: Cardiac is a fun connection for you. Your first exposure to the head of the bed was a heart transplant. Cardiac anesthesia is required at Emory, and Emory Healthcare is a big cardiac center. If someone asks what part of anesthesia you're most curious about, cardiac is an honest, natural answer.
COACH: Now, the numbers. Emory's outcomes for twenty twenty-three through twenty twenty-five: a ninety-eight percent first-time pass rate on the national certifying exam, a hundred percent overall pass rate, a hundred percent job placement, and ninety-five percent retention. In plain words, they get people through, they get them certified, and they get them hired.
COACH: Admissions numbers, so you know where you stand. Last cycle, Emory got about twenty-four hundred applications. Around seven hundred were complete and verified. They interviewed about a hundred and sixty people, and made forty offers, for a class of up to forty-two. So getting an interview already puts you in roughly the top quarter of complete applicants. Of the people they interview, roughly one in four gets an offer. It's competitive, but the interview is now the whole game.
COACH: Their current first-year class, the class of twenty twenty-eight, has thirty-nine students. The average G P A is three point seven five overall, and three point seven science. Your three point nine four is above both. Their average G R E quant is one fifty-nine point two. Your new quant score is one sixty-one. That's above their average, so if anyone brings up your G R E, you're in a strong spot. The average age at matriculation is twenty-four. And about three quarters of the class is from the Southeast. You'd be one of the few westerners in the room. That's not a problem. It just means your answer to "why Emory, why Atlanta" needs to be real.
COACH: The program's academic program director is Dr. Katie Monroe, a C A A herself, with a P h D. She did a podcast interview this February about Emory's program. You don't need to name-drop her. But if she's in the room, it's good to recognize the name.
COACH: Now, your interview day. You check in at eleven forty-five Eastern. That's nine forty-five Utah time, so your body clock is on your side. Up to three hours. Business attire. Eight candidates per session. The day includes a program information session, formal interview breakouts, a group Q and A, Q and A with current students, a tour of the learning space, and light refreshments.
COACH: Here's the thing about a day like that. You're being watched the whole three hours, not just in the breakout. The student Q and A is not a break. Students often give feedback. Be the person who's curious, who asks good questions, who's warm with the other seven candidates. Don't compete with them out loud. In a care team program, how you treat your peers is part of the evaluation.
COACH: Let's talk about your questions for them. You'll have at least two chances to ask, in the group Q and A and with students. Have five ready. Here are good ones for you.
COACH: For faculty. One. How are the out-of-town rotations in the final year assigned, and how much input do students have? Two. Since clinical rotations start alongside classes in the first four semesters, what do the students who thrive in that first year do differently? Three. How is the simulation center used across the program? Is it mostly early, or does it continue into the clinical year? Four. Where do most of your graduates end up working, and how many stay in Georgia?
COACH: For current students. One. What does a normal week look like in your first semester? Two. Where do most students live, and how bad is the commute to clinical sites? Three. What surprised you most about the program? Four. What do you wish you'd done the summer before starting? That last one is a great question, because it shows you're already thinking like a student there.
COACH: And one question to avoid. Anything you could have answered by reading their website. Don't ask how long the program is.
COACH: Now, let's build your "why Emory" answer. Three parts. Something about the program, something about the place, and something about you that fits.
COACH: The program: it's one of the two places the profession started, it has excellent outcomes, and it puts you in the O R early, alongside the classroom, which is how you learn best.
COACH: The place: Atlanta is a huge, diverse clinical environment. Georgia is a licensure state with a deep C A A workforce. And there's a large Spanish-speaking community in metro Atlanta. Buford Highway, the corridor known for its Latino and immigrant communities, runs just a few miles from Executive Park. Your Spanish is a patient safety tool in a pre-op interview, and Atlanta is a place where you'd actually use it.
COACH: You: you and your wife want this move. You're both excited about an East Coast adventure, and the South fits you better than cold places. Keep this light. One sentence. And a warning from your earlier writing work: don't frame Atlanta as a stepping stone back to Utah. Frame it as where you want to train.
COACH: Okay. Proctor.
