COACH: Now, the soft spots. Most won't come up. But if one does, the rule is the same every time. Short, honest, no excuses, then pivot to what you did about it. Twenty to thirty seconds. Never longer. Long answers make a small thing look big.
COACH: Soft spot one. Your G R E. You took it once in May without studying, and got a one fifty-seven in quant. Then you retook it in July and got one sixty-four verbal and one sixty-one quant. Their incoming class averaged one fifty-nine point two in quant. So you're above their average now. If it comes up: "The first time, I honestly didn't prepare. I retook it, prepared properly, and brought my quant up four points." Done. That's a story of taking something seriously, not a weakness.
COACH: Soft spot two. Outstanding prerequisites. You're taking biochemistry this fall, and calculus and physics two this winter. You graduate in April twenty twenty-seven, well before an August start. If asked: "I'm in biochem now, and I'll finish calculus and physics two this winter. I graduate in April." Say it plainly. If you're doing well in biochem, say so. It's the class most related to pharmacology.
COACH: Soft spot three. A couple of prerequisites are technical edge cases. Your biology one course didn't include a lab, and your physics one came from A P credit, so it shows as a pass. These are paperwork questions, and it's unlikely they come up in a breakout. If they do: "I know those are a bit unusual. I'm happy to take whatever you'd want me to before starting." That's the whole answer. Showing flexibility is the point.
COACH: Soft spot four. No research. Lots of applicants don't have research. If it comes up, don't apologize. Say your experience has been clinical and patient-facing, and then show you read the literature. For your N S U application, you summarized a twenty twenty-six Anesthesia and Analgesia study on gastric ultrasound in patients taking G L P one drugs like Ozempic before anesthesia. That's a perfect thing to mention. G L P one drugs slow stomach emptying, which raises aspiration risk, and it's a hot topic in anesthesia right now. Three points you remember from that paper. The ultrasound only modestly predicted a full stomach. There were no actual aspiration events, so they measured a substitute outcome. And they screened over fourteen thousand patients down to about three hundred, so selection bias is a real question. Being able to critique a study like that tells them you can read research, even if you haven't done it.
COACH: Soft spot five. You found this profession recently. Some interviewers will notice that most of your experience was built for P A. Your answer is that you found it late, but you checked it out carefully. You shadowed in two different settings, your brother-in-law is an anesthesiologist, and you read up on the field. Late, but deliberate.
